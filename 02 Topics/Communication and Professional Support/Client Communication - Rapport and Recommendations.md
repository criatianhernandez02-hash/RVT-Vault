# Client Communication - Rapport and Recommendations

> [!note] Built with Claude on 2026-11-09 for [[IVT L04 - Communication in the Veterinary Field]] and for work
> Open this on your phone before a shift. The phrase table is meant to grow: when something works in a room, add it. Say *"role-play: <scenario>"* to Claude to rehearse.

> [!abstract] In one sentence
> Clients follow recommendations from people who listened first: ask open questions, reflect back what you heard, name the feeling, then recommend clearly, in chunks, tied to *their* pet, and check in. The skills are learnable and the research is consistent about which ones matter.

## The skills that are proven to matter
Veterinary schools teach communication with the **Calgary-Cambridge Guide**, adapted from human medicine. Its four core skills, and what each sounds like:

| Skill | What it is | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Open-ended questions** | Questions that can't be answered yes/no; they get the real story | "What's been going on with Bella?" · "Tell me more about the vomiting." · "What else have you noticed?" |
| **Reflective listening** | Repeating back the gist, in your words, so they know you heard | "So the limping started after the hike on Saturday and it's worse in the mornings." |
| **Empathy statements** | Naming the feeling or the effort, without agreeing or fixing | "That sounds scary to watch." · "You've clearly been paying close attention to him." |
| **Nonverbal awareness** | Eye contact, open posture, sitting when you can, calm hands, your tone, and reading theirs | Kneel to the pet's level, face the client, phone and screen down while they talk |

Three **structure** skills from the same guide make recommendations land:
- **Signposting**: say what comes next. "First I'll get his weight and temperature, then the doctor will come in and go over everything."
- **Chunk and check**: give two or three pieces of information, then pause and check. "Does that make sense so far?"
- **Ask-tell-ask**: ask what they already know ("What have you heard about heartworm?"), tell them the key points in small pieces, then ask what questions they have or have them repeat the plan back.

Two frameworks you'll see in the lesson and in practice-management articles:
- **The 4 Es** (adapted for vet med in 2006): **Engage** (greet, connect, agenda), **Empathize** (acknowledge feelings), **Educate** (explain in plain words), **Enlist** (invite them into the decision). That's an exam room visit in four words.
- **AAHA's compliance equation, CRAFT**: Compliance = Recommendation + Acceptance + Follow-Through. A clear recommendation, reinforced by the whole team, accepted by the client, with follow-up. Half of CRAFT happens at the technician's end: the reinforcement and the follow-through.

A detail worth knowing: when researchers asked dog and cat owners which skill mattered most to them, the answer was **reflective listening**. Being heard outranks being impressed.

## Rooming a patient: the first three minutes
1. **Before you open the door:** glance at the chart. Pet's name, sex, age, reason for visit, last visit. Say the pet's name right.
2. **Enter and connect:** eye contact, smile, your name and role. "Hi, I'm Cris, one of the technicians. And this must be Bella." Greet the pet second, on its terms (see below).
3. **Set the agenda:** "Today we've got Bella in for her annual and vaccines. Anything else you want to make sure we look at while you're here?" This one question prevents the "oh, and also…" at checkout.
4. **Open question, then listen:** "How's she been doing?" Then stop talking. Nod, let the silence work, jot notes without looking away for long.
5. **Reflect and empathize:** "So she's been drinking more and you've had to refill the bowl twice a day. That's a good catch; most people wouldn't notice."
6. **Signpost:** "Here's what happens next: I'll get her weight and vitals and ask a few questions, then Dr. Lee will come in, examine her, and go over the plan with you."
7. **Hand off well:** give the doctor the two-sentence story *and* the client's own words. Clients hate repeating themselves.

## Making a recommendation they can say yes to
The pattern: **this pet + the team's recommendation + why, in plain words + what it involves + a check-in.**

> "Because Bella's outside a lot and we've had leptospirosis cases in the area, **we recommend** the lepto vaccine for her. It's a two-shot series; the first one is today and the booster is in three to four weeks. What questions can I answer about it?"

What makes it work:
- **"We recommend"**, not "do you want": it's the practice's standard of care, delivered by the whole team, not a menu item. The doctor decides the medicine; you reinforce it with the same words.
- **Tie it to *this* pet**: lifestyle, age, breed, what the owner told you five minutes ago. Generic advice sounds like upselling; specific advice sounds like care.
- **One idea at a time**, then check. Three vaccines, a fecal and a dental estimate in one breath becomes "no thanks."
- **Benefit before procedure**: what it protects against or prevents, then what it involves.
- **Ask permission before educating**: "Would it help if I explained how heartworm prevention works?" People listen to information they agreed to hear.
- **Offer choices inside the recommendation**, not instead of it: "We can do all three today or split them over two visits; both work."
- **Money, straight and early**: "The estimate for today is about $210; I can walk you through it." Shame around cost loses more clients than cost does.

## Say this, not that

| Instead of | Try | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Do you want the heartworm test?" | "We recommend the heartworm test for every dog yearly; here's why for Max…" | A question invites "no"; a recommendation invites "tell me more" |
| "You should have brought him in sooner." | "I'm glad you brought him in today. Let's figure this out." | Blame ends the conversation; relief opens it |
| "It's just a vaccine, it's fine." | "Most dogs are a little sleepy for a day; here's what to watch for and when to call." | Dismissing a worry teaches them not to voice the next one |
| "That's expensive, I know." | "The estimate is $210. Want me to go through what's in it?" | Apologizing for the price makes it sound optional |
| "Calm down." | "I can see this is really upsetting. I'm here, take your time." | Naming beats commanding |
| "Did you give the medication?" | "How did the medication go at home?" | Open question gets the truth ("he spat out half") |
| "The doctor will explain." | "The doctor will go over the plan; the short version is…" then signpost | Silence feels like withholding |
| Medical jargon ("PU/PD", "CBC") | "Drinking and peeing more"; "a blood test that counts her cells" | They can only agree to what they understand |

## When they hesitate (vaccines, diets, cost)
1. **Acknowledge, don't argue:** "You've obviously thought about this." Arguing with a belief hardens it.
2. **Ask what they've heard:** "What have you read about the rabies vaccine?" You can't correct what you haven't heard, and asking is respectful.
3. **Ask permission to share:** "Can I tell you what we see in the clinic?"
4. **Share in chunks, then check:** two facts, pause.
5. **Leave the door open:** "Whatever you decide, we'll take good care of her. Here's what we'd watch for in the meantime." Many "no" clients become "yes" clients at the next visit if the first visit felt safe.
6. **Hand the medicine to the doctor:** risk, benefit and alternatives are the veterinarian's to explain; your job is to make sure the client feels heard and knows the question is welcome.

## Respect for the pet (it's part of the relationship with the client)
Owners judge you by how their animal responds to you. Fear Free basics: let the pet approach you; crouch sideways rather than loom; offer treats (ask first); quiet voice; cats stay in the carrier with the top off, or on a towel, not dumped on a cold table; stop and reassess when the animal escalates instead of "just getting it done." Say what you're doing out loud: "I'm going to lift his lip to check his gums." Narrating calms the owner and slows you down in a good way.

## After each room: a 10-second self-check
- Did I ask an open question and then actually stop talking?
- Did I reflect something back in my own words?
- Did I name a feeling once?
- Did the client know what was happening next the whole time?
- Did I use "we recommend" and tie it to this pet?
