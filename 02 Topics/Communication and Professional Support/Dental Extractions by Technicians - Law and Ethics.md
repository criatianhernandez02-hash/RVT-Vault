# Dental Extractions by Technicians - Law and Ethics

> [!note] Research done with Claude on 2026-11-09 for [[IVT L02 - Contributing to a Veterinary Team]]
> Law and position statements as found on the organizations' own pages and California's regulations. The conclusion the lesson asks for is yours; the facts are here so you can cite them. One warning up front: California's rule on this is **changing right now** (see the legal section), so check the Board's page before you quote it at work.

> [!abstract] In one sentence
> California law lets an RVT extract teeth under a veterinarian's direct supervision; the AVMA, AAHA, AVDC and AVDT all draw the line tighter, from "non-surgical only, under direct supervision" (AVMA) to "never, regardless of state law" (AVDT), because extraction is oral surgery with real ways to go wrong.

## The legal side: California

**Current regulation (16 CCR 2036(b)).** An RVT may perform **dental extractions**, but only under the **direct supervision** of a licensed veterinarian. Direct supervision means the veterinarian is *physically present at the location* where the task is done and *quickly and easily available*, and the animal has been examined by a veterinarian as good practice requires (16 CCR 2034). Indirect supervision (veterinarian off site, reachable by phone) is **not** enough for extractions. So in California the legal answer to "may I?" is **yes, with conditions**: a veterinarian on the premises, an examined patient, and a veterinarian who has chosen to delegate it (the veterinarian stays legally responsible for the task, 16 CCR 2035).

**What's changing: AB 516 (2025).** A bill sponsored by the CVMA and shelter groups, AB 516 (Kalra), was reported as chaptered on **October 6, 2025** (Chapter 371, Statutes of 2025). Its text says RVTs may perform dental care procedures, *including tooth extractions*, "under the supervision" of a California veterinarian, without the word "direct". The Veterinary Medical Board's staff noted the Board must now review 16 CCR 2035, 2036 and 2036.5 to conform them to the amended statute. Until that rulemaking is done, the regulation's **direct-supervision** requirement is the rule to follow in practice. One tracking site labeled the bill inconsistently, so confirm its status on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov before relying on it.

**What California does not say.** The regulation says "dental extractions" with no distinction between a mobile single-rooted tooth and a surgical extraction that requires sectioning the tooth or removing bone. The organizations below draw exactly that distinction, which is where law and ethics part ways.

## The ethical side: what the four organizations say

| Organization | Position on technicians extracting teeth | How it compares with California law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AVMA** (policy: *Veterinary Dentistry*) | Extraction is part of veterinary dentistry, "to be performed by licensed veterinarians in accordance with their state veterinary practice act." Appropriately trained veterinary healthcare workers may perform certain **non-surgical** oral and dental procedures, **only under direct supervision** and in accordance with state regulations. Extractions, when justified by the oral exam, are done under anesthesia | **Overlaps** on direct supervision and on deferring to state law; **stricter** in treating extraction as the veterinarian's procedure, with technicians limited to non-surgical work |
| **AAHA** (2019 *Dental Care Guidelines for Dogs and Cats*) | The credentialed technician does the awake and anesthetized oral exams, intraoral radiographs, cleaning, regional nerve blocks where indicated, and charting. "Because extractions are considered oral surgery, they should not be performed by veterinary technicians" | **Stricter** than California: no technician extractions at all, regardless of supervision |
| **AVDC** (American Veterinary Dental College, position statement on *Veterinary Dental Healthcare Providers*) | Extraction is included in the practice of veterinary dentistry. It is appropriate to delegate maintenance dental care and certain dental tasks to a technician (impressions, radiographs, non-surgical subgingival scaling and debridement that does not alter tooth structure). The AVDC does **not** support its Diplomates training technicians or assistants to perform **oral surgery, including surgical extraction techniques** | **Stricter** on surgical extractions; quieter on simple extractions of mobile teeth, which is the gap California's wording leaves open |
| **AVDT** (Academy of Veterinary Dental Technicians, *Extraction Position Statement*) | "Extraction of teeth is oral surgery and should be performed by a licensed veterinarian." Technicians should **not** perform extractions, credentialed or not. The Academy states its position **supersedes state practice acts that allow technician extractions**, and a member found performing extractions is dismissed and loses the VTS (Dentistry) credential permanently | **Strictest**, and the only one that explicitly says its standard outranks a permissive state law. Legally it can't override California's regulation, but it does bind its own members |

**Overlaps:** all four treat extraction as surgery or as the veterinarian's domain; all four want technicians doing the radiographs, cleaning, charting and nerve blocks; all four defer to, or at least reference, state law for what is *legal*. **Differences:** California permits what AAHA and AVDT prohibit; the AVMA and AVDC draw a surgical/non-surgical line that California's text doesn't; and the AVDT alone claims its ethics beat the statute.

For context, the gap isn't unique to California. The AAVSB's model regulation would let technicians perform single-root extractions that don't require sectioning tooth or bone; Washington requires *immediate* supervision for technician extractions; Ontario's regulator prohibits delegating extractions entirely. Textbooks often describe the cautious middle: if state law and the veterinarian allow it, a technician performs only non-surgical extractions of extremely mobile teeth.

## Why the organizations set a higher bar than the law
- **Extraction is surgery, and it fails in surgical ways:** fractured roots left behind, jaw fractures in small dogs and cats with advanced periodontal disease, hemorrhage, oronasal fistulas, damage to neighboring teeth. Deciding *whether* a tooth comes out needs radiographs and clinical judgment, which is diagnosis, and diagnosis belongs to the veterinarian.
- **Welfare first:** a technician who can extract a mobile tooth may be handed the next, less mobile one. A bright line protects patients from scope creep in a busy practice.
- **Professional integrity and liability:** the organizations speak for a whole profession and set standards that hold across all fifty states; a practice act is a floor set by one state's politics. A technician who follows the stricter standard protects their license, their employer, and the profession's credibility.
- **Specialists know the failure modes best:** the AVDC and AVDT are the dental specialists; their caution comes from seeing the complications.
- **The counter-argument, which the lesson wants you to weigh too:** access to care. California's law and AB 516 exist because there aren't enough veterinarians, and well-trained RVTs doing routine extractions under direct supervision get more animals treated. Shelters sponsored the bill for that reason. An ethical stance can be sincere and still have a cost.
